01 · Bodies

SMPL is the human side of the archive.

A URDF describes a robot’s rigid links and joints. SMPL describes a deformable human body: a skeleton, a skinned surface, learned shape variation and pose-dependent surface corrections. It provides a common human representation for fitting people and expressing motion that can later be transferred to a robot.

SMPL’s basic layout has 24 joints. SMPL-H adds articulated hands; SMPL-X extends the body with hands and expressive face controls. Their parameter layouts differ, so an importer must know which model produced a file. SMPL project ↗ · SMPL-X project ↗

Joint conventions

A number needs a named index set.

URDF joints have names, types, parent/child links, local frames, axes and limits. The author—often the robot manufacturer—chooses those names and coordinates. URDF does not prescribe a universal numerical motor-ID order. ROS JointState pairs named joints with values; controller and simulator vector orderings must be checked separately.

This site gives independently controllable robot joints display numbers starting at 1, grouped by body region and then sorted by joint name. Fixed joints and mimic joints have no independent sliders. These numbers help operate the map; they must not be sent to a robot as motor addresses.

Human model indices are part of a parameter layout.

SMPL-family models use a defined pose-joint order. Those indices differ from mesh vertex numbers, OpenPose/COCO landmarks, fingertips added by a joint selector, or dense facial landmarks. A model can output more landmarks than it has articulated pose joints.

The demonstrators label pose joints from 0 and export their names, parents, authored rest positions and rotation convention. Matching indices is only one step: rest frames and coordinate conventions must also agree.

Collect on the target robot.

Teleoperation can give you aligned camera views, joint state and the actual commands used by your controller. Record calibration, control rate, task and episode boundaries. Keep evaluation tasks, scenes or objects separate from training so repeated demonstrations do not inflate results.

05 · Transfer

Different bodies share principles, not identical controls.

A character starts with a mesh and a joint hierarchy. Skin weights let vertices follow multiple bones; animation changes the bones over time. A rigid robot link follows its link transform. A stylized person may add twist bones and facial blend shapes. A quadruped has different limb roles, spine motion and contact timing.

Retargeting begins by aligning units, up/forward axes and reference poses. Map corresponding joints or chains, transfer relative movement into the target’s frames, then use inverse kinematics to preserve important reaches and contacts while respecting target proportions and limits.

A human walk cannot simply become a dog gait by renaming bones. Animal motion data, gait design or a suitable controller must supply the missing behavior. SMAL offers a related parametric model for several quadruped families; it is not a universal creature rig.

What changes when the target is a robot?

Matching a hand position is only the kinematic step. The robot also needs a controller that respects joint velocity and torque limits, self-collision, balance and contact forces. Inspecting a pose in the viewer does not simulate those constraints.

A useful next website feature would show the source motion, target motion, joint correspondence and contact or limit errors together. That would make the transfer understandable before exporting it to a simulator.
